# Introduction

Thank you for taking the time to speak with me.

I am a Computer Engineer from Brazil, and throughout my career I have worked at the intersection of software, firmware, hardware and real-world systems.

My story really started long before university, through a lifetime of technical hobbies, experimentation and curiosity.

# Technical Childhood and Hobbies

Since childhood I have been involved with mechanics, fabrication, electrics, model aircraft, and others.

I have always enjoyed understanding how thiongs and systems work, improving them and building things myself.

Looking back, many of the skills I use professionally today originated from decades of hands-on building and experimentation.

# Computer Engineering and Graduate Research

My Computer Engineering education provided a broad foundation in computing, electronics, automation, control systems, mathematics and engineering.

While still an undergraduate student, I joined a graduate research environment focused on Industrial Informatics and Flexible Manufacturing Systems.

From early on, I had the opportunity to work alongside researchers, Master's students and PhD candidates on real research and development projects.

That experience had a major influence on the way I approach engineering problems and exposed me to complex multidisciplinary systems much earlier than would normally be expected during an undergraduate program.

It was during this period that I became involved in the development of a large manufacturing simulation environment, a project that would continue for several years and later become closely connected to my Master's work.

---

# Master's Degree and Industrial Simulation

I later completed a Master's degree in Industrial Informatics and Flexible Manufacturing Systems.

The manufacturing simulation environment that I had started working on during my undergraduate years continued to evolve throughout my Master's program.

The project combined manufacturing algorithms, production-system modeling and advanced computer graphics to simulate complex manufacturing environments.

The system ran on HP-UX and IBM AIX workstations and represented a significant software engineering effort for the time.

My Master's research focused on control aspects within manufacturing systems, complementing the broader simulation work being developed by the research group.

This period provided deep exposure to simulation, modeling, manufacturing systems, computer graphics and large-scale software development, experiences that would continue to influence my engineering approach throughout my career.

# The Photographic Minilab Project

One of the most interesting projects involved developing a Brazilian photographic minilab system.

The challenge was to digitize negatives, analyze images and calculate correction parameters for optical and chemical photographic processes.

We had to combine image processing, mathematics, software engineering, optics and physical process control.

Technically it was an extremely multidisciplinary challenge.

# Telecommunications at Scale

Telecommunications later became a major focus of my career and of VOXEL itself.

Initially, I worked with teams developing software for Siemens telecommunications platforms and switching systems.

As mobile telecommunications expanded, our focus increasingly shifted toward inter-operator settlement and reconciliation systems.

This was long before concepts such as Big Data, cloud computing, distributed processing frameworks or modern large-scale data infrastructures became common.

Mobile operators needed to exchange, validate, consolidate and reconcile massive volumes of operational records generated by their network infrastructure.

We developed systems capable of processing tens or hundreds of millions of records per day, supporting settlement processes between operators where accuracy, traceability and reliability were essential because they directly impacted financial transactions.

However, the challenge went far beyond software development.

VOXEL maintained teams responsible for product development, product evolution, customer-specific customizations, deployments, training and operational support.

Our engineers regularly worked directly with telecom operators across the Americas, Europe and Asia-Pacific.

Projects involved understanding customer requirements, designing solutions, developing customizations, performing deployments, training operational teams and supporting production environments.

The operational pace was intense.

Telecom operators typically wanted solutions immediately, and production issues often required rapid responses.

As a result, we maintained an organizational structure capable of quickly mobilizing technical resources when necessary.

In many ways, it operated like an engineering SWAT team.

When a problem emerged somewhere in the world, we had to rapidly understand the situation, identify the right specialists, organize travel if needed and deliver solutions under significant time pressure.

International travel became a normal part of our operation.

There was almost always a deployment, a customer request, a production issue, a customization project or a critical deadline somewhere.

For many years it was an extremely demanding environment that often felt close to a 24x7 operation.

Looking back, those years provided invaluable experience in large-scale mission-critical systems, international customer engagement, technical leadership, teamwork, rapid problem solving and operating under sustained pressure.

Many of the challenges we faced would later become associated with Big Data and large-scale distributed systems, but we were solving those kinds of problems years before those terms became widely used.

# UAVs and the UAV Outback Challenge

Aviation and model aircraft remained lifelong passions.

Around 2009 I became involved with early open UAV autopilot platforms and eventually assembled a team of 3 persons to participate in the UAV Outback Challenge Search and Rescue competition.

The aircraft was designed specifically for the mission requirements of the competition.

We integrated and extended ArduPilot, modified firmware, modified MAVLink behavior, integrated sensors, implemented redundancy strategies, safety mechanisms and mission-specific logic.

Initially I modified Mission Planner directly.

Later I developed an intermediate architecture that allowed us to intercept and inject MAVLink messages while remaining compatible with upstream software changes.

Everything was self-funded and developed in parallel with our professional activities.

Although we ultimately lost the aircraft shortly before the competition, the engineering experience remains one of the most intense multidisciplinary projects I have ever participated in.

# Recent Years – XMobots

More recently I worked at XMobots, one of Latin Americas's leading UAV companies here at Brazil.

My official consultant specialist role was related to Sensors and Embedded Systems, but in practice my work became much broader than that.

Initially, much of my involvement was around evaluating, integrating and validating sensors and payloads for UAV platforms.

This included thermal cameras, military-grade imaging systems, agricultural sensors, remote sensing equipment and other airborne payloads.

I was involved in technical analysis, integration, testing, validation and operational evaluation of those systems.

Over time, however, I believe people realized that my background extended far beyond sensors alone.

As that happened, I increasingly became involved in a wide variety of technical challenges across different areas of the company.

Sometimes the problem involved sensors.

Sometimes communications, embedded software, firmware, system integration ,mission requirements, propulsion systems, fuel tanks and fuel delivery systems, radio-control firmware and communications links.

Sometimes operational issues observed during testing.

Sometimes the challenge was not even technical implementation, but understanding which technology should be adopted in the first place.

I frequently participated in the technical evaluation of technologies and suppliers from the United States, Europe and China.

This included analyzing specifications, discussing technical details directly with vendors, evaluating competing solutions and helping determine which sensors, cameras or subsystems best matched product and mission requirements.

Many times the work involved laboratory and automation for bench testing, Power measurements, Electrical characterization, Propulsion testing, Thrust measurements ,Performance analysis, Investigation of anomalous behavior, Validation of embedded systems and payloads.

What I found particularly interesting was that the most difficult problems rarely belonged to a single discipline.

Often a problem that initially appeared to be related to a sensor turned out to involve communications, software, integration, operations or some interaction between multiple subsystems.

I was frequently brought into situations where the real source of the problem was unclear and where a broader systems perspective was required.

That felt very natural to me because it was consistent with the rest of my career.

Whether in simulation, telecommunications, industrial systems, UAVs or embedded systems, I have often found myself working at the boundaries between disciplines.

I enjoy understanding the complete system, identifying root causes and helping connect knowledge from multiple engineering domains to reach practical solutions.

In many ways, my experience at XMobots reinforced something that has been true throughout my entire career:

My strongest contribution is often not expertise in a single component, but the ability to understand complete systems, recognize interactions between subsystems, anticipate problems before they emerge, and help ensure reliable, safe and mission-effective operation.

# What Decades of Engineering Have Taught Me

Over the years, I think the most valuable thing I developed was not expertise in a particular technology.

Technologies change.

Industries change.

Tools change.

What remains useful is the ability to understand complex systems.

To anticipate problems before they become critical.

To recognize interactions between subsystems.

To identify root causes.

To work effectively with specialists from different disciplines.

To remain productive when requirements are unclear and solutions are not obvious.

I have spent much of my career working on systems where reliability, real-world constraints and operational success mattered.

Those experiences taught me to think beyond individual components and focus on the behavior of the complete system.

Another lesson I learned is that successful engineering projects are ultimately about people.

Over the years, whether as an entrepreneur, project leader, consultant or professor, I learned the importance of identifying strong contributors, building trust, helping teams work effectively together and creating environments where people can do their best work.

Teaching for more than fifteen years reinforced this perspective even further. It taught me how to communicate complex ideas, mentor people with different backgrounds and recognize potential in future engineers.

They also taught me persistence.

Many of the most rewarding projects I have participated in required months or years of effort, overcoming technical obstacles, uncertainty and changing requirements.

I have learned that difficult engineering problems are rarely solved by a single technology or a single specialist.

They are usually solved by teams that can integrate knowledge across multiple domains and keep moving forward until a solution is found.
